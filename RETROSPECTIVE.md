# Retrospective — For the Omar Who Started Week 1

You went into this thinking "personal site" meant putting your CV online and
being done in an afternoon. It didn't turn out that way, and that's the whole
point of what I want to tell you.

## What I set out to do

The goal looked simple: get a portfolio live that says who I am — Communication
& Electronics Engineering, embedded systems, AI, the Arduino projects, the
training I'd done at AMIT, ST Smart, NTI, ITI — and give people a way to
actually reach me. I figured the hard part would be the design. It wasn't.

## What actually changed

The hard part turned out to be everything *after* the page looked finished.
I built the site, it looked clean, and I was ready to call it done. Then I
actually tried to break it — clicked every link instead of assuming they
worked, and found the CV download was a dead 404. That one stung a little,
because it's exactly the link a recruiter clicks first. Nobody would have
told me. I'd have just never known why nobody replied.

That was the real shift: I stopped treating "it looks right" as the finish
line and started treating "I tried to break it and wrote down what happened"
as the finish line. The empty "Posts & capstone work" section on the site
is still empty — but now it's empty on purpose, stated plainly, instead of
being an accidental gap I was hoping nobody would notice. There's a real
difference between those two, and I didn't understand that difference in
Week 1.

The other thing that changed is how I think about search and sharing. I
never thought about what happens when someone pastes my link into LinkedIn,
or what shows up when someone Googles my name. Adding meta tags and a
favicon felt like a small, almost cosmetic task. It wasn't — it's the
difference between my site looking like a real, findable thing versus a
blank preview card that makes people not click at all.

## What I'd build next

Right now the site is a static identity page — accurate, but static. The
next real version needs actual content behind the "Posts & capstone work"
section: a write-up of this FlyRank capstone itself, and probably a proper
project page for one of the Arduino builds (the obstacle-avoidance robot,
most likely) with photos and a short video, not just a bullet point on a CV.
I'd also want the contact form to show a real confirmation message instead
of silently clearing — a small thing, but it's on my known-limitations list
for a reason.

## The three most transferable things I learned

**1. "Working" and "verified" are not the same claim.** A feature can look
done and still be broken in a way you'll never notice unless you deliberately
try to break it yourself — empty inputs, wrong inputs, double-clicks, a
browser you don't normally use.

**2. Naming a limitation is stronger than hiding it.** I used to think an
unfinished section made me look unfinished. Writing "nothing published yet"
plainly, instead of leaving a mysterious gap, reads as someone who knows
exactly where their own work stands — which is more credible, not less.

**3. Using AI well means staying the one who verifies.** Claude helped me
draft meta tags, write the favicon, and structure the hardening report. But
I was the one who clicked every link on the real, deployed site and confirmed
the fixes actually worked before I called anything done. That division — AI
drafts, I verify against reality — is the actual skill this track was
teaching, more than any specific line of HTML.

— Omar
